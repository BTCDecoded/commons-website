Abstract

Bitcoin's consensus rules are enforced by the network. Development of the software people run is still a maintainer process.

This paper describes two pieces of work. BLVM is an independent node checked against a written spec. Bitcoin Commons publishes the rules for how that software changes, and those rules can be forked.

The work is organized around six core engineering tracks in the public BTCDecoded organization (spec, consensus, protocol, node, SDK, and governance), plus supporting repositories for packaging, blvm-spec-lock verification, and the blvm-commons Governance App. Engineering detail lives in technical documentation.

1. Introduction

Bitcoin solved Byzantine consensus between strangers (Nakamoto, 2008) but ignored consensus between developers. The network's substantial market capitalization demands institutional maturity matching its technical excellence.

The original cypherpunk developers focused on eliminating trusted third parties in transactions but inadvertently created trusted parties in development. Bitcoin Commons addresses Bitcoin's most critical vulnerability: governance asymmetry between technical consensus and development coordination.

1.1 The Talent Bottleneck: Orders of Magnitude and Sources

Bitcoin development draws on multiple hard domains simultaneously (C++, applied cryptography, distributed systems, security engineering, economics/game theory, and open-source governance). Each extra domain narrows the pool. Using conservative, sourced baselines and clearly labeled assumptions, the analysis below estimates the rarity of a contributor who combines these competencies and is available to work on Bitcoin:

Assumptions and sources:

  • World population baseline: ~8.1B (UN DESA, World Population Prospects, 2022 Rev.)
  • Global developers: ~30M-47M (range spanning widely cited industry estimates, incl. SlashData and similar studies)
  • C++ share of developers: ~15%-25% (range spanning major annual developer surveys)
  • Adult numeracy (problem-solving proficiency): on the order of 10%-20% globally (OECD PIAAC cross-country evidence; global extrapolation is approximate)
  • Bitcoin Core maintainers: single-digit individuals; contributors: hundreds (public repo statistics)

Rarity funnel (indicative, overlapping, not strictly independent):

  • Strong college-level math (calculus/linear algebra): 3%-5% of population, resulting in 240M-400M
  • Professional developers: ~30M-47M (subset, separate baseline)
  • C++/systems competency: 15%-25% of developers → 4.5M-11.8M
  • Applied cryptography + Bitcoin protocol literacy: 1%-2% of C++ devs → 45k-236k
  • Distributed systems/P2P networking depth: 30%-50% → 13.5k-118k
  • Security engineering mindset (memory safety, adversarial thinking): 20%-30% → 2.7k-35k
  • Economics/game-theory literacy: 30%-50%, resulting in 0.8k-17.5k
  • Open-source governance (review culture, consensus norms): 10%-30%, resulting in 80-5k
  • Communication/reliability under public scrutiny: 30%-50% → 24-2.5k
  • Availability/alignment to work on Bitcoin: 10%-30% → ~2-750

Interpretation:

  • Even with generous ranges, the intersection yields on the order of dozens to a few hundred globally available individuals with the full stack to work reliably on Bitcoin's most sensitive layers.
  • Public data corroborates scarcity at the tip: Bitcoin Core has hundreds of credited contributors but only a small, rotating single-digit maintainer set. This human bottleneck contrasts with the cryptographic abundance at the consensus layer.

Of 132 Bitcoin Core authors with at least five pull requests and a quality score of at least 0.3, 41 were still active and 91 were not (Bitcoin Governance Research). Counted another way, 7,971 people have contributed since 2010, 72 sustain a merge record, and 5 hold the keys that merge to the main branch.

2. Problem Statement

Technical Reality

Bitcoin's consensus rules are embedded in hundreds of thousands of lines of reference-client C++ without a canonical mathematical specification the ecosystem standardizes on. In Core 32.1 the rules that decide whether a block or transaction is valid are 10,122 lines, 7% of 151,004 lines of implementation. The same five merge keys gate the other 93%: the code that applies and relays those rules, and the wallet, interface, and network code. The dominant reference implementation carries enormous practical weight over how the network evolves. On November 3, 2026, reachable crawls of the Bitcoin network showed about 89% running Bitcoin Core and about 10% running Knots, down from about 22.5% Knots in the 2025 to mid-2026 window. Knots still shares about 95% of that codebase, so about 99% of those nodes ran one implementation lineage. Safe independent implementations and systematic verification remain exceedingly difficult under reference-client-as-spec; Commons publishes layered specifications to address that gap.

Items with broad agreement have still waited for years. Wallet and node separation, UTXO set commitments, and networking upgrades are in that set. The stall is structural.

Governance Reality

Bitcoin's development governance relies largely on informal social coordination. At network scale, consequences for violations are rarely systematic, dispute resolution is ad hoc, and authority is difficult to audit. Across the full Bitcoin Core history, the top three merger roles account for about 81% of merges. Authorship concentration is about 0.85 on a scale where 1 means one actor, and review concentration is about 0.92. The network itself stays permissionless. The development process is gated by legitimacy, funding, and who can merge. The system is vulnerable to capture through relationships rather than rules. Network analysis reveals structural misalignments between technical development and social infrastructure, creating coordination gaps that prevent effective governance (Hough, 2025). These patterns reflect the paradox of embeddedness in network structures, where relationships can inhibit coordination and reinforce existing power dynamics (Uzzi, 1997). Funding may not flow to projects with strong grassroots activity, and "rich-get-richer" dynamics reinforce existing patterns rather than enabling competition. Commons addresses these gaps through cryptographic enforcement of multisig thresholds, mandatory review periods, and transparent accountability mechanisms.

Zero-review self-merges fell from 42.2% in 2017 to 4.0% in 2026, a partial year. 2016 is not the baseline. It is the first year GitHub review objects exist in the dump, so that year's rate mixes a missing instrument with the later practice. Zero-review merges of other authors' pull requests fell from 32.4% in 2017 to 8.8% in 2026. The self-merge fall may reflect a maintainer norm, tooling, or how review is recorded. Which of those it is remains an open question (Bitcoin Governance Research, findings/TEMPORAL_ANALYSIS_REPORT.md, note dated 2026-11-11, stored series in analysis/findings/data/temporal_analysis.json at commit 1225a188, repository c526cd03). The self-merge series measures maintainers reviewing work that already reached review. It does not describe the filter before review. Crawford and Ostrom (1995) treat a statement with a sanction as a rule and a statement without one as a norm. Writing is not the line. Acheson (1988) documented unwritten territorial rules whose sanction was cutting traps. Core's review practice states no sanction, so this paper classes it as a norm. The norm held among insiders. The case for writing a rule down is legibility and accountability to people outside the group, who cannot watch the norm. Commons has written rules, and enforcement of those rules is not on.

Historical Context

Early leadership transitions and public governance disputes showed that developer coordination can fail without institutional enforcement. Andresen (2014) and Hearn (2016) raised governance concerns from inside development; academic researchers documented the structural gap but offered no implementable remedy (De Filippi & Loveluck, 2016).

Scale Considerations

Bitcoin's growth from early stages to multi-trillion dollar scale requires institutional reform. The next crisis, whether regulatory capture, internal conflict, or novel attack classes, will not wait for the community to develop governance solutions reactively.

3. Theoretical Framework: The Triple Foundation

Bitcoin Commons synthesizes three distinct theoretical frameworks, each addressing weaknesses in the others to create governance architecture stronger than any single approach alone.

Framework 1: Elinor Ostrom - Commons Governance

Elinor Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics for proving that shared resources don't inevitably collapse into chaos or capture (Ostrom, 1990). Her research documented principles for governing commons without central authority across centuries of real-world examples.

Ostrom's (1990) Eight Principles.

  1. Clearly defined boundaries. Who may use the shared resource, and who decides.
  2. Congruence between rules and local conditions. Stricter rules where the stakes are higher.
  3. Collective-choice arrangements. The people bound by a rule can take part in changing it.
  4. Monitoring. Participants can see the conditions and the decisions.
  5. Graduated sanctions. Penalties on someone who breaks a rule scale with the violation.
  6. Conflict-resolution mechanisms. Disputes have a forum short of leaving the institution.
  7. Minimal recognition of rights to organize. Outside authorities do not deny the community's right to make its own rules.
  8. Nested enterprises. Each layer governs itself within the constraints of the layer beneath it.

What This Provides. A researched design for shared resources, evidence that decentralized governance can work, and coordination without a single office.

Four levels of rules

Ostrom's principles are not the same question as where a decision sits. A repository and a deployment can each carry four levels. Ostrom (2005) includes the metaconstitutional level, the rules for making constitutional rules. This paper reads that level as purpose and legitimacy. Operational rules are what the software does on a run, including defaults it ships. Collective-choice rules are how those operational rules change. In this design that is the tier and layer schedule. Constitutional rules are who has standing to change the schedule. That is the maintainer and keyholder rosters. The meta-constitutional level is purpose and legitimacy. It is not a signature. Legitimacy rests on adoption and exit. The Compact says authority flows from users, and that it means nothing until users adopt it. These sentences describe the design. Enforcement of the signature schedule is not on.

Relay policy is the worked example. People can agree that a function rejects a class of transactions and still disagree on whether that default is required by the Compact's settlement purpose or is a setting maintainers may change. A review period does not settle that disagreement. The design now requires a written interpretation in the public record, open for the comment period the applicable tier already has. The interpretation is not a veto, and it does not amend the Compact. BLVM ships a mempool switch that rejects spam, and that switch defaults to off. The filter parameters have their own defaults when the filter runs. An operator can override them. The shipped values still have weight. The shipped default stays on the tier that change already has. It is not moved to a 5-of-5 review, because that would let one key stall it. The authors publish a rationale tied to the Compact and say whether the purpose is contested. If it is contested, they publish an interpretation and leave it open for the comment period that tier already uses. The design does not decide which reading of the purpose is right, and it does not stop this client's default from differing from another client's.

Where the design does not fully hold

These are projections of the design once activation is complete. They are not a claim that enforcement is on today. A threshold-sized group can change the governance rules. Removing the publication requirement is a Compact amendment, because that requirement derives from the Transparency principle. The Compact was not amended here. One missing signature can stall a 5-of-5 change, and two can stall a 6-of-7 change. A signed refusal makes that stall attributable. Silence does not. A non-conforming action, including one by a human administrator, is reversed by a quorum of governance nodes. That reversal is Designed, and it is not on today. The company that operates the host can still override the organization. Operators then rely on a signed release they can check without the host. A standing personal owner account is not part of the design. One node acting alone does not stand. AI output is evidence, not the conformance check. Competitive response and user-experience work are not emergency criteria. A governance fork checks a rule set the operators refuse, and it also splits the maintainers. Signed publication raises the cost of breaking an undertaking only when a later action cites the record. Contribution volume and a review-guidance flag do not grant standing to merge. A change that alters which findings that guidance treats as blocking is a governance-rule change. None of these sentences adds a signature count.

Framework 2: F.A. Hayek - Spontaneous Order

Friedrich Hayek's Austrian economics provides the competitive discovery mechanism that enables governance evolution rather than rigid design.

Hayek's Core Insights.

  • Dispersed Knowledge Problem. No central planner can know what's needed because knowledge is distributed across many actors.
  • Competition as Discovery. Competition reveals information that couldn't be known in advance.
  • Spontaneous Order. Best institutions emerge through evolution, not top-down design.
  • Markets Need Infrastructure. Competition requires actual alternatives to compete.

What This Provides. Justification for avoiding central planning, competitive governance discovery, and institutions that evolve through market signals.

Framework 3: Bitcoin - Cryptographic Enforcement

Bitcoin's innovation provides the enforcement tools that make decentralized governance work at scale without trusted parties.

Bitcoin's Core Principles.

  • Don't Trust, Verify. Cryptographic enforcement replaces social trust.
  • Permissionless Innovation. Anyone can build without asking permission.
  • Exit Rights. Fork option provides ultimate check on power.
  • Decentralized Control. No single point of authority.

What This Provides. Tools for enforcing rules without trust, proof decentralized systems work at scale, and a model for implementing Hayek's principles digitally.

The Triple Synthesis

Each framework covers a hole in the others. Ostrom's commons depended on social pressure, and Bitcoin replaces that pressure with a check anyone can verify. Hayek's competition needs real alternatives, and Ostrom's institutions are how more than one model can exist under rules. Bitcoin settled agreement on the chain and left agreement on the code informal. Hayek's discovery and Ostrom's rules are the design for that layer. The result is a design. Enforcement is not on.

Cryptographic Polycentrism: The Governance Model

Bitcoin Commons implements Cryptographic Polycentrism, a governance model that creates multiple centers of power with clear boundaries. The design uses cryptographic checks rather than social pressure alone. Those checks are not on. This approach applies Ostrom's principles, and it leaves room for more than one implementation to be tried.

Cryptographic Polycentrism differs from traditional polycentrism (multiple decision centers) by using cryptographic enforcement to make boundaries mathematically verifiable rather than socially negotiated. Where traditional governance relies on trust and reputation, Cryptographic Polycentrism uses multisig thresholds, mandatory review periods, and three-layer verification providing real-time transparency and immutable proof to create boundaries a quorum of governance nodes is designed to enforce once production governance is on. Human repository administrators are shut out of governance-relevant actions. A non-conforming action is reversed when two of three nodes in a written set agree. An unsigned merge does not become a signed release. This is Designed. Enforcement is not on today. The company that operates the host remains outside that quorum.

This model enables multiple implementations to compete (Hayek's discovery mechanism) while maintaining clear institutional boundaries (Ostrom's principles) through cryptographic proof (Bitcoin's innovation). The result is a design that aims to be competitive and accountable. Enforcement is not on.

Informal governance can work at smaller scale and lower complexity; at multi-trillion dollar scale, social enforcement alone struggles to keep pace with coordination demands and accountability expectations. Commons encodes Ostrom's principles through a cryptographic check. This is Designed. Enforcement is not on today. The mapping below describes how each principle is written in this architecture.

Mapping The Principles to Implementation

The table is the four-source map from the principles figure: Ostrom, Hayek, Bitcoin, and Cypherpunk. The bullets after it are Ostrom's eight, including congruence and nested enterprises, which that figure did not list as their own rows. Signature checks, Nostr publication, timestamp anchors, and the contributor ladder are designed. They are not on.

The modular architecture writes Ostrom's eight principles as a cryptographic check. This is Designed. Enforcement is not on today. The bullets below are the account of each principle in this design.

Principle 1. Clearly Defined Boundaries.

  • Layers. The base consensus layer stays outside the module layer. Modules cannot modify consensus code paths.
  • Contributor jurisdiction. Standing, when the contributor ladder is in force, applies to code a person has a merged record in. Designed, not enforced.

Principle 2. Congruence Between Rules and Local Conditions.

  • Thresholds scale with stakes. Signature requirements and review periods run from 2-of-3 and 14 days at the extension layer to 6-of-7 and 180 days at the constitutional layers, and 365 days for consensus-rule changes.
  • Status. Those numbers are configured in the governance repository. Cryptographic enforcement of them is designed, not on.

Principle 3. Collective-Choice Arrangements.

  • Binding rules. Rules that bind every participant change through the governance tiers.
  • Exit, not a tally. An operator composing a module stack binds only that operator. Hirschman (1970) called this exit. Adoption figures report what operators chose, and no governance decision tallies them.
  • Implementation. Module composition is declarative (node and SDK configuration). Operator-facing UX may include a CLI, config files, RPC, and an optional UI as tools mature.

Principle 4. Monitoring.

  • The public record. BTCDecoded Intelligence is a separate product for this job. It searches the public coordination record and returns an answer with its source. Sign-in is GitHub or Nostr. Payment is Lightning. The source is what a reader checks.
  • Cryptographic transparency. Governance actions are designed to be signed, published to Nostr, and anchored with OpenTimestamps, so any participant can audit them without trusting a monitor. A merge that lacks its required signatures is designed to be refused at the gate. Three-layer verification is the mechanism. Not enforced today.

Principle 5. Graduated Sanctions.

  • Penalties on violators. Sanctions attach to a participant who breaks a contributor obligation, and they scale from formal censure to loss of standing. They are specified with the contributor-standing ladder. Not enforced today.
  • Not the signature schedule. Multisig thresholds are congruence (Principle 2), not sanctions.

Principle 6. Conflict-Resolution Mechanisms.

  • Modules. Conflicts between modules resolve through operator configuration. No central arbiter. Module conflicts do not threaten consensus.
  • Shared layers. A contested change can be paused for inquiry. Inquiry is a pause, not a veto, and it is designed, not enforced. A governance fork is the resolution when a dispute cannot be settled inside the rules.

Principle 7. Minimal Recognition of Rights to Organize.

  • What the principle asks. Outside authorities leave the community's rule-making alone. Commons cannot obtain that recognition in every jurisdiction.
  • Substitutes. Keyholders distributed across jurisdictions, revenue that does not depend on one funder, and a ruleset any participant can fork.

Principle 8. Nested Enterprises.

  • Layers inside one ruleset. Modules operate inside the consensus boundary. The extension, application, implementation, and constitutional layers each carry their own thresholds inside one ruleset.
  • Neighboring products. Plebly, BTCDecoded Intelligence, and Commons Pool are not layers of Commons. Each governs its own work, outside the merge set. None of them changes consensus rules, and none of them counts toward a signature.

The eight principles are the account of this design. Cryptographic Polycentrism is how Commons encodes boundaries, congruence, nesting, and auditability. Plebly, BTCDecoded Intelligence, and Commons Pool are separate products, purpose-built so funding, reading the public record, and non-custodial mining can follow those principles. They do not replace the ruleset. Enforcement of the merge gate is not on.

Related products

These products sit beside Commons. They were built so a principle has somewhere to operate. They are not the ruleset, and they are not the node.

Plebly lists public Bitcoin development work and takes donations into on-chain escrow. The coins sit at an address a donor can check. Plebly's keys are its own. A donation is a way to bear cost. It does not change a tier, and it is not standing.

BTCDecoded Intelligence is cited search over Bitcoin's public coordination record: IRC, mailing lists, GitHub, forums, specs, and papers. An answer comes back with its source. Access is GitHub or Nostr, paid in Lightning, and connected over MCP. The Orange Paper remains the normative layer. A note on a public pull request is an argument a person can check. It is not a signature, and it is not on the merge path.

Commons Pool is non-custodial mining: a found block can pay the miner's address. It is built. It is not a live mainnet pool. Using it is a pool's choice, and that choice is not a vote.

4. Technical Solution: The Orange Paper

Problem

Bitcoin's consensus rules lacked a canonical mathematical specification industry-wide (reference-client-as-spec remains the practical default). The consensus-critical denial-of-service vulnerability CVE-2018-17144 existed undetected for years: exactly the class of error formal verification and layered testing aim to prevent. Forking the reference client keeps its codebase and its merge process. Independent clients that dropped the UTXO set, or that stopped active development, do not give operators a second consensus implementation.

Solution

The Orange Paper provides a formal mathematical specification of Bitcoin's consensus protocol through AI-assisted extraction from Bitcoin Core's codebase. The specification includes:

  • Mathematical foundations (set theory, cryptographic primitives, network protocols)
  • State transition functions (block validation, transaction validation, consensus rules)
  • Economic model (mining rewards, fee calculations, difficulty adjustment)
  • Security properties (Byzantine fault tolerance, Sybil resistance, double-spending prevention)

Benefits

  • Safe alternative implementations: Independent implementations can verify against mathematical specification
  • Formal verification: Consensus-critical code is checked against the Orange Paper through BLVM Specification Lock, property tests, and CI; see Formal Verification
  • Fewer unnamed rules: a written spec makes a consensus mistake easier to point at

AI-Assisted Extraction Methodology

The Orange Paper uses AI-assisted extraction from Bitcoin Core's codebase to formalize consensus rules. This approach:

  • Analyzes Bitcoin Core's codebase to identify consensus-critical code paths
  • Extracts mathematical relationships from implementation details
  • Creates formal specifications that are independent of specific code structure
  • Enables verification that specification matches actual network behavior

Proof Maintenance and Specification Quality

The formal verification process includes ongoing maintenance so the specification stays aligned with the implementation. A code change is checked for drift against the Orange Paper, the spec is updated when the wording and the code diverge, and the lock is run again.

Commons publishes a layered specification: the Orange Paper (formal math), CONSENSUS_SPEC (numbered RFC 2119 rules mapped to code), and PROTOCOL (extended constants and formulas). Entry points are at thebitcoincommons.org/spec.html. blvm-spec-lock ties blvm-consensus to these artifacts; drift and coverage tooling tracks alignment between spec wording and lockable contracts. Details: Orange Paper, Formal Verification.

Parallel independent specification efforts reinforce the same goal from complementary formalisms. Toby Sharp's Hornet project demonstrates that a minimal, executable consensus specification and domain-specific language can sync mainnet independently of the reference client (Sharp, 2025; hornetnode.org), strengthening the case for Commons' layered Orange Paper and CONSENSUS_SPEC approach. Keagan McClelland's btc-verified pursues the same correctness goal through machine-checked Lean 4 proof leaves over serialization, transaction identifiers, and merkle commitments, validated against mainnet golden vectors (McClelland, 2026; github.com/ProofOfKeags/btc-verified). Where Hornet emphasizes an executable implementation-neutral specification, btc-verified emphasizes theorem-checked byte-layer foundations; Commons' spec-lock contracts and differential testing complement both. The choice of notation is a governance choice. A reader of the Orange Paper needs the mathematics and the domain. A reader of a Lean proof needs Lean. A reader of a custom language needs that language.

Empirical consensus alignment (differential testing and fuzzing)

Mathematical specification and spec-lock contracts bound what the rules ought to be. Differential testing and fuzzing ask whether the running implementation agrees with the dominant reference implementation on real chain history and on adversarial inputs. Methodology, current depth, and CI status are documented in technical documentation. Prefer that over this document for live coverage figures.

Together these layers complement formal methods: specs and contracts for specification fidelity; replay and fuzzing for empirical alignment with observed network behavior.

BLVM Architecture

BLVM (Bitcoin Low-Level Virtual Machine) is an implementation checked against the Orange Paper. The paper is the written form of the consensus rules, so more than one implementation can be compared to it. Stack layout: System Overview, Consensus Overview.

Single Source of Truth. All consensus logic resides in blvm-consensus. Upper tiers (blvm-protocol, blvm-node) delegate validation calls with no duplicate implementations. Path dependencies ensure changes propagate immediately through Rust's type system.

Because every consensus decision flows through blvm-consensus, verification and tests at that layer bound behavior for the whole stack. Upper tiers delegate validation and cannot bypass that layer. Proportional thresholds are safe in this design because those boundaries are enforced. A layer 5 change can take a cheaper path because a module runs in its own process and cannot touch the consensus crate. In a codebase without that boundary, every change has to be treated as if it might touch consensus. Bitcoin Core's libbitcoinkernel is the shared library for its consensus and validation code (bitcoin-core/doc/files.md). The wallet and the GUI are not supposed to depend on it, and networking stays in the node (bitcoin-core/doc/design/libraries.md). An implementation built on it depends on Core's consensus code. The exception inside Commons is node code that builds the state consensus is given. Consensus is pure, so it is only as correct as those inputs. The UTXO store, reorg handling, and block connection can diverge from another client without a change to the consensus crate. Those paths use the consensus-adjacent tier already written. Other code above that class mostly risks liveness.

5. Architectural Solution: Modular Governance

BLVM is the written spec and the node. Commons is the published governance. The modular architecture is where they meet. Commons distributes authority through Cryptographic Polycentrism, defined below.

Commons distributes authority through cryptographic multisig requirements and mandatory review periods, designed so that no single keyholder can approve a merge. This Cryptographic Polycentrism model creates multiple decision centers (repository layers, action tiers, emergency tiers) with mathematically enforced boundaries. Approval and blocking are different costs, as Section 6 sets out.

Two-Layer Architecture

The modular architecture consists of two layers that transform governance conflicts from political battles into architectural choices:

Layer 1: Mandatory Consensus (Base Node)

  • Bitcoin's consensus rules, unchangeable without network agreement
  • Cryptographically enforced, defines what "Bitcoin" means
  • Examples: block validation, transaction validation, fork choice rules

Layer 2: Optional Modules (Extension System)

  • User-controlled features that can be enabled or disabled
  • Communities can fork/modify/compete, user choice determines winners
  • Examples: Lightning Network, Taproot Assets, privacy enhancements, Stratum/mining-pool interfaces, optional merge mining where enabled

Revenue Model

  • Self-sustaining development through optional modules
  • Channels inside Commons, with no single stream required: optional modules (operational today), module registry and distribution (not live yet; discovery and fees when deployed), governance announcement zaps (Lightning), and optional merge mining for pools that choose it. Commons Pool is the separate product for that mining path. It is built. It is not a live mainnet pool.
  • Plebly is separate as well. Donations go to on-chain escrow for listed work. A donation bears cost. It is not a module fee, and it is not a vote.
  • Use-based revenue tracks who benefits. A checkable donation is a different object from a grant concentrated in one funder.

Module Isolation

Optional features load as modules: separate processes with strict boundaries, not compile-time features baked into the base node:

Process Isolation Mechanisms:

  • Each module runs in its own process with isolated memory
  • Modules communicate through a documented ModuleAPI (subprocess IPC)
  • Base node validates all blocks using Orange Paper specification regardless of enabled modules
  • Module state is separate from consensus state (UTXO set)

API Boundaries:

  • Modules interact with the base layer only through documented interfaces
  • No direct access to consensus functions or core data structures
  • Module failures cannot propagate to the base node

What modules CANNOT do: Modify consensus rules, alter block validation, cause network splits

What modules CAN do: Provide optional services (relay, mining interfaces, Lightning, ZMQ, etc.) without affecting base-node stability

Containment Strategy

The modular architecture satisfies both camps simultaneously:

  • "Don't Change Bitcoin" Camp: Gets pure Bitcoin base layer with no modifications
  • "Make Bitcoin Useful" Camp: Gets optional features through modules
  • Miners and pools: Stratum V2 connectivity to the ecosystem; optional merge mining only where operators enable it, not the primary sustainability bet

The Module System IS The Governance System: Instead of governing through committees deciding features, Commons governs through architecture enabling choice. The module system isn't just technical: it's the governance mechanism itself, implementing exit through user configuration (Hirschman, 1970), Hayek's competitive discovery through module competition, and Bitcoin's permissionless innovation through fork-ability. Collective choice over rules that bind every participant remains with the governance tiers in Section 6.

Fragmentation Analysis:

Governance forks preserve the consensus layer while allowing governance changes. Users can fork governance rules while keeping the same Bitcoin consensus. This is the ultimate accountability mechanism. Alternate full-node clients (for example Bitcoin Knots) can share one chain when validation rules stay aligned. Knots is mostly the same codebase, so that coexistence is not a second implementation. Reported shares move by window and method. The structural point is one chain, not a market-share figure.

Forking Process: Governance rulesets can be exported as versioned, signed packages containing action tiers, maintainers, cryptographic proofs, and semantic versioning. This enables users to fork governance while maintaining compatibility and verification.

Optional modules are operational today (loadable processes via ModuleAPI). A module registry for permissionless listing and automated validation is part of the distribution model but not live yet.

6. Cryptographic Governance Enforcement

Commons implements Cryptographic Polycentrism through three complementary verification layers (distinct from repository layers). The Governance App is the merge gate. Nostr carries signed status. OpenTimestamps anchors the record. Each layer checks the others. None of the three is enforced in production today.

Layer 1: Governance App (Merge Control). Designed to, when production governance is activated, enforce merge policy. The Governance App is designed to be the merge gate. It would check signatures and review rules and refuse a merge that does not meet them. Cryptographic merge enforcement is off today. A GitHub App is one way to run that gate. The requirement is the gate, not the forge.

  • The Governance App is designed to validate multisig requirements (varies by layer: 2-of-3 to 6-of-7)
  • A self-hosted runner behind WireGuard VPN is designed to validate signatures using secp256k1. On GitHub, that runner is a GitHub Actions runner. Another forge would use its own hook.
  • Once production governance is on, governance bots hold administrator access and human repository administrators are shut out of governance-relevant actions. Two of three governance nodes in a written set must each conclude that an action conforms, or that a reversal conforms, before it stands. A non-conforming action, including one by a human administrator, is reversed. Anyone may run a node. Only the written set binds. This is Designed. Enforcement is not on today.
  • Signature validation is designed to happen before merge approval
  • Designed to require explicit cryptographic approval from multiple maintainers before any merge
  • No-self-merge: a contributor cannot cryptographically approve their own pull request; the Governance App is designed to reject self-signed merges. Not enforced today.

Layer 2: Real-Time Transparency (Nostr). Designed to publish signed status updates (binary hash, config hash, recent merges, health metrics) to Nostr relays so subscribers can verify server integrity. Cadence and alerting are deployment parameters, not guarantees about any particular live deployment.

Layer 3: Immutable Proof (OpenTimestamps). Designed to anchor canonical registry snapshots and critical events (key rotations, deployments) to the Bitcoin blockchain for dispute resolution and independent verification. Provides court-admissible evidence where applicable.

Cross-Layer Verification:

Three independent layers verify governance actions and each other. Risk at one layer does not compromise the others. This defense-in-depth approach ensures governance integrity even if one verification method is compromised.

Emergency Response

Commons implements a three-tier emergency system for critical security and network issues. Emergency tier activation is separate from action tier classification (see below): emergency keyholders declare the class; merge rules and review periods adjust until automatic expiration.

Activation (all classes): 5-of-7 emergency keyholders. Merge policy while active varies by class: network-threatening incidents use the fastest path (zero review, lower maintainer merge threshold); less urgent classes use longer review and stricter merge thresholds. Authoritative parameters: governance repository (emergency-tiers.yml).

Critical Emergency (Network-threatening vulnerabilities):

  • Merge signatures while active: 4-of-7
  • Review period: 0 days (immediate response)
  • Maximum duration: 7 days (no extensions)
  • Examples: Consensus-critical denial-of-service bugs, consensus fork risks, P2P network DoS vulnerabilities, remote code execution

Urgent Security Issue (Serious security issues):

  • Merge signatures while active: 5-of-7
  • Review period: 7 days
  • Maximum duration: 30 days (1 extension allowed)
  • Examples: Memory corruption vulnerabilities, privacy leaks, crash exploits, privilege escalation

Elevated Priority (Important but not critical):

  • Merge signatures while active: 6-of-7
  • Review period: 30 days
  • Maximum duration: 90 days (2 extensions allowed)
  • Examples: important bug fixes that are not security issues, and performance degradation of a running node. Competitive response, ecosystem compatibility, and user-experience work stay on the ordinary tier. A policy default and a governance-rule change cannot use this tier.

The system scales response speed and review strictness to severity, not a single monotonic rule that stricter always means worse. Network-threatening issues get the fastest merge path; important-but-non-critical work uses longer review and higher merge bars. All emergency actions require post-mortem documentation and automatic expiration.

An emergency expires on its own, and the lowered merge rule expires with it. A change already merged under that rule stays merged. The repository does not bind an activation to one patch, does not require later ratification at the ordinary threshold, and does not bar an emergency from covering governance rules or key sets. Five emergency keyholders can therefore turn a constitutional change into a 4-of-7 merge with no review, and the result remains after the emergency ends. Closing that gap is an open design decision.

Security Architecture: Push-Only Design

The layout below is the GitHub deployment of the Governance App. Another forge would keep the same rule, outbound-only checks before a merge, and would change the host.

Security Architecture Details:

  • No HTTP Endpoints: Governance servers have no incoming HTTP endpoints (minimal exposure surface)
  • VPN Isolation: Servers communicate outbound only through WireGuard VPN
  • Self-Hosted Runner: On this host, a GitHub Actions runner behind WireGuard VPN validates signatures
  • Data Flow: Server to the repository host (push) to Nostr (publish) to Bitcoin (anchor)
  • Public Read Access: Repository, Nostr relays, Bitcoin blockchain (read-only for public)

Multisig Threshold Details

Authoritative configuration. Signature thresholds, repository layer definitions, action tier names, review periods, and emergency parameters described in this section are illustrative of the governance design. Security-class overrides (for example consensus-critical or cryptographic paths) may impose stricter requirements than the tables below; see action-tiers.yml in the governance repository. Authoritative numeric requirements and rule text live in that repository (YAML and related artifacts) and may change as policy evolves. This PDF is not the source of truth for merge policy; when in doubt, consult the repo.

Commons writes two tables: repository layers (where the change is made) and action tiers (what type of change is being made). They are not one numbered system. When both apply, the repository's combination rule selects the longer review period and the larger signature count. That count is not always the stricter condition when the layer and the tier name different key sets.

Repository Layers (Where):

  • Layer 1-2 (Constitutional): Orange Paper and Consensus Proof - 6-of-7 signatures, 180-day review (365 days for consensus changes)
  • Layer 3 (Implementation): Protocol Engine - 4-of-5 signatures, 90-day review
  • Layer 4 (Application): BLVM Node - 3-of-5 signatures, 60-day review
  • Layer 5 (Extension): SDK and modules - 2-of-3 signatures, 14-day review

Action Tiers (What):

  • Tier 1 (Routine Maintenance): Bug fixes, documentation, optimizations - 3-of-5 signatures, 7-day review
  • Tier 2 (Feature Changes): New RPC methods, P2P changes, wallet features - 4-of-5 signatures, 30-day review
  • Tier 3 (Consensus-Adjacent): Changes affecting consensus validation code - 5-of-5 signatures, 90-day review
  • Tier 4 (Emergency Actions): Critical security patches, network-threatening bugs - 4-of-5 signatures, 0-day review (with post-mortem required)
  • Tier 5 (Governance Changes): Changes to governance rules themselves - 5-of-5 signatures, 180-day review

Emergency PRs vs emergency activation. Action Tier 4 classifies pull requests for critical security work under normal maintainer multisig. Emergency tier activation (above) is a separate declaration by emergency keyholders that switches merge rules until expiration. Both can apply together; see emergency-tiers.yml and action-tiers.yml in the governance repository.

Combination rule:

When both a layer requirement and a tier requirement apply, the governance repository takes the longer review period and the larger signature count. For example:

  • A routine bug fix (Tier 1) in the Protocol Engine (Layer 3) takes 4-of-5 signatures and a 90-day review
  • A new feature (Tier 2) in the SDK (Layer 5) takes 4-of-5 signatures and a 30-day review
  • A consensus-rule change (Tier 3) in the Orange Paper (Layer 1) takes 6-of-7 signatures and a 365-day review

Six signatures out of seven is a larger count than five out of five and a weaker condition when the five must be unanimous and the sets differ. The repository's combination rule does not yet require both named sets to sign. Whether a change that meets both a layer rule and a tier rule must satisfy each set, rather than a single merged count, is an open design decision. The lists above are the numbers. The YAML is authoritative.

All signatures verified using secp256k1 (same curve as Bitcoin). The Governance App is designed, when production governance is activated, to take a host action only after two of three governance nodes have published the same mechanical conclusion. A node that acts alone is reversed by the other two. Credentials that can install or remove the app are held by that quorum, not by a standing personal owner account. The company that operates the host can still override the organization. Operators accept a release only when the required maintainer signatures attest to it and the build is reproducible, so an unsigned merge does not become a signed release. A reproducible rebuild check is Built for the blvm and blvm-sdk base variants in the release workflow, and that workflow is started by hand. Maintainer attestation of a release is Designed and is not in that workflow. Enforcement of the quorum is not on today. A GitHub App is one host for the gate.

A high threshold makes approval expensive and blocking cheap. Where a missing signature counts as a no, two constitutional keys can stall a 6-of-7 change, and one key can stall a 5-of-5 governance change or a 7-of-7 security-critical change. A stalled change is a capture outcome in its own right.

The specified remedy separates the two costs. A block is a signed reject of one change, it names a reason, and it expires at the end of that change's review unless the same keyholder renews it. Silence is not a reject. On a consensus-rule change, one explicit reject forces the full review, because a bad merge can split the network and a delay leaves the current rules in place. Two renewed rejects can keep holding that change. On a change to the governance rules or to who holds a key, the bar is a supermajority short of unanimity, and a keyholder whose own seat is the subject is recused. These reject, expiry, and recusal rules are specified. They are not in the governance repository, and they are not enforced.

Shared-layer decisions are binary, one specific change against the status quo. Arrow (1951), Gibbard (1973), and Satterthwaite (1975) showed that no ranked voting rule over three or more alternatives can be non-dictatorial, Pareto efficient, and independent of irrelevant alternatives, and that every such rule rewards some voter for misreporting. A binary decision falls outside those results. They apply again when two rival changes and the status quo are in play together, which is why a reject attaches to one change and does not become a standing veto over the layer.

Mandatory review periods prevent rushed merges. The dual-dimensional system (layers and tiers) keeps routine changes in critical repositories at the higher approval threshold.

Contributor standing (proposed)

Beyond repository-layer multisig, a contributor-standing ladder complements merge policy: ledger-derived standing, crate-scoped participation, no-self-merge enforcement at the merge gate, and an Inquiry mechanism as a pause, not a veto, for contested changes. Described in a separate specification to be published when cryptographic governance activates. Not enforced today. The live governance repository defines maintainer multisig and tiers only. Its maintainer lists are placeholders, one set per layer plus a separate emergency set, and the production roster has not been set. The thresholds above settle how keyholders decide. Who holds the keys settles who decides. Tier 5 at 5-of-5 means any incumbent can veto a change to the key set, including their own removal. Selection, rotation, and recusal are outlined only, and they are not enforced.

7. Economic Sustainability

The Funding Gap

Only $8.4 million from 13 organizations supported Bitcoin Core development in 2023, while the network reached a $2 trillion market cap (Hough, 2025). That is roughly 0.00042% of market cap, or about $4.2 million of protocol funding per $1 trillion of native market cap. On the same normalization, Ethereum protocol funding in the cited window is about $125 million per $1 trillion.

The funding and market-cap figures are snapshots from the cited work (2023 funding year; market cap at the time of that analysis). They illustrate scale mismatch; they are not live dashboards.

In raw dollars, Bitcoin Core's grant year is not the smallest budget in open source. It is larger than the organizations that keep several widely used projects running, and smaller than a protocol treasury's development line or the Ethereum figure above.

The Apache Software Foundation reported about $2.4 million of revenue in FY2024. The Python Software Foundation reported about $4.1 million of revenue in 2024. The Rust Foundation reported about $4.3 million of revenue in 2024, and about $413,000 of that went out as grants to maintainers. The Zcash Foundation's 2024 expenses were about $8.0 million: about $1.0 million of protocol work and about $5.1 million of community grants. Its block-reward development fund ended at the November 2024 halving. Polkadot's 2024 treasury report counts about $32 million of development spending, of which about $6.7 million was the protocol and SDK. The same report counts about $133 million of treasury spending in total, mostly outreach and business development, so that larger number is not a protocol budget. The Linux Foundation reported about $221 million of revenue in 2024 for the whole foundation. That figure covers many projects, so it is not a kernel budget and it is not on the chart.

Self-Sustaining Revenue Models

Use-based module revenue is the Commons channel that tracks who benefits. Optional modules are operational today. Sustainability is multi-channel: module revenue, registry-based distribution (when the registry is live), Lightning zaps, and optional mining-side revenue. None is assumed to carry the project alone. Plebly is the separate product a donor uses. A foundation grant is the concentration the charts above measure.

Module registry and distribution: Planned permissionless discovery, quality signals, and developer alignment with project revenue; not live yet.

Governance announcement zaps: Lightning zaps attached to governance announcements fund governance operations directly and tie community support to transparent announcements.

Plebly: A separate product. It lists public development work and takes donations into on-chain escrow. The address is public. A donation does not buy a tier.

Optional merge mining: Commons Pool is the separate non-custodial mining product. It is built, and it is not a live mainnet pool. For pools that enable merge mining, that revenue is supplemental, not a prerequisite for the economic model.

These self-sustaining models align incentives with usage and adoption, making the system less vulnerable to capture than grant-dependent funding models. Commons positions as infrastructure for multiple implementations, not a reference-client replacement: success is measured by ecosystem health and adoption of the model, not market share.

8. Failure Modes & Mitigations

Governance Capture

Risk: Keyholder collusion or compromise

Mitigation: Multi-jurisdictional keyholders, transparent operation, fork-ready design. Informal governance at scale is easier to capture through relationships than architecture with enforced boundaries. Capture can also take the form of paralysis. A high threshold makes blocking cheaper than approving. At 6-of-7, two keys can stall a constitutional change. At 5-of-5, one key can stall a governance change.

Regulatory Pressure

Risk: Authorities pressure keyholders to implement backdoors

Mitigation: Keyholders are distributed so that no single jurisdiction holds enough keys to approve a change at any layer. Blocking is a lower bar. A jurisdiction that can compel two constitutional keyholders can stall a 6-of-7 change, and one that can compel a single keyholder can stall a 5-of-5 governance change. A keyholder who blocks a change records a signed refusal. Silence is not that record. That refusal is Designed, and it is not enforced today. A forkable ruleset is the remedy for a stall that persists. Modular containment limits the blast radius of a compelled change that does merge.

Technical Risks

Risk: Module consensus bugs, complexity explosion

Mitigation: Module isolation, formal verification, security audits

Social Risks

Risk: Community rejection, fork wars, reputation attacks

Mitigation: Focus on substance, build alternatives, let market decide; not asking permission, let code speak, coalition provides proof

Ultimate Protection

9. Conclusion

How the reference client changes is still a maintainer process. BLVM is an independent implementation of a written spec. Commons publishes the rules for changing the software, using Ostrom's principles for a commons and a signature schedule maintainers can fork. Enforcement of that schedule is not on. Activation begins as BLVM moves from alpha to beta, on no fixed timeline, and it waits on adoption as well as on the text of the rules. A shipped policy default stays on the tier it already has, and a contested reading of the Compact's purpose takes a published interpretation rather than a silent default.

The design applies published work in economics, social science, and cryptography to a written rule set. Cryptography is how a check can be run without trusting the person who states the rule. That check is not on. Infrastructure is what gives more than one implementation somewhere to run. Modularity and the freedom to fork are how discovery stays possible. None of this is a completed proof that the design works in production.

The foundation and architecture exist in public repositories. The project's future depends on community participation.

The repositories are public, and you can check yourself.
